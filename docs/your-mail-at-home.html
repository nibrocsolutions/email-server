The hard part

A house is a fine room and a poor front door

The Pi can keep letters. That part is ordinary. Other companies decide whether to hand you a letter, or accept one from you, based on the door.

1

The mail door is often locked

Home internet companies often block the door computers use for mail. If that door is shut, nobody can deliver, and you cannot send.

2

The address moves, or is shared

A home address can change. Sometimes many houses share one address, so there is no door of your own on the street.

3

The name on the door does not match

Receivers ask, “Who lives at this address?” They want the answer to be mail.example.com. Home providers almost never publish that.

Four checks, before any software

1
Is the address shared?

Compare the address your router shows with the address a “what is my IP” site shows. If they differ, the world cannot knock on your house.

2
Can outsiders reach the mail door?

From a phone on cellular data, outside your wifi, see whether your house answers on the mail door.

3
Can the Pi send outward?

From the Pi, see whether it can open the mail door of a big mail company. Many home lines block this too.

4
Will they print your name?

Ask the internet company to publish your address as mail.example.com. This is called reverse DNS. Home companies rarely agree.

Sam writes to Alex

1

Sam writes the letter

On a phone, in Gmail. The address is alex@example.com. Sam taps send.

2

Gmail looks up the street

The address book says mail for example.com goes to mail.example.com, the front desk. That line in the book is called an MX record.

3

The letter arrives at the front desk

Gmail knocks on the public mail door. The desk accepts the letter because it is addressed to someone at example.com.

4

It crosses the private tunnel

The desk walks the letter through the hallway to the Pi. The hallway is called WireGuard. Your house can open it from the inside.

5

The Pi files it

A junk-mail sorter scores it. The letter is stored on the disk at home. Alex’s phone picks up a copy the next time the app is opened.

What Alex notices.

A new message, in the same kind of app as always. The tunnel, the desk, and the Pi stay out of sight.

Follow one letter

Alex writes back

Sending is the direction that gets people into trouble. Gmail trusts a letter when the address it came from matches the name on the door.

1

Alex taps send

The phone connects to a sending door and gives a password. Strangers cannot use this door. The password proves it is Alex.

2

The Pi takes the letter

A copy is filed at home, in Sent. The Pi prepares the letter for the outside world.

3

The Pi stamps a seal

The seal is called DKIM. Later, Gmail can check the seal and see the letter really comes from example.com.

4

The front desk carries it out

The Pi hands the letter to the desk. The desk delivers it from the public address, the one whose name matches mail.example.com.

5

Gmail checks the guest list

The guest list is called SPF. It says the front desk is allowed to send for example.com. The house address is not on that list, on purpose.

A brand-new door is a stranger.

The first letters to Gmail sometimes land in spam. Ordinary mail, sent and answered like a person, is how that door earns a reputation. Bulk mail is a different project and does not belong here.

Three doors

A computer has numbered doors

A port is a numbered door. Mail uses three. Each one has a different visitor.

25

Public mail slot

Other companies deliver here. The door accepts letters for people at your domain, and turns away mail that asks to be passed on to someone else.

587

Your sending window

Alex’s phone uses this door to hand over a new letter. It asks for a username and a password. The trip is locked so nobody on the way can read it.

993

The reading room

The phone uses this door to fetch letters. It also asks for a password, and the trip is locked. This way of reading is called IMAP.

In this plan, all three doors are on the front desk.

The desk passes each visitor through the tunnel to the Pi. The Pi is the one who files the letter, checks the password, and keeps the mailbox. The lock on the trip is a certificate: a document that says “this really is mail.example.com.”

Words you will see

The same ideas, with their real names

You already met these in the story. This page is the translation, for when a settings screen uses the short name.

Server

A computer that stays on and waits for requests. Here, the room where mail is kept.

Raspberry Pi

A small, cheap, quiet computer. Ours lives at home and holds the mailbox.

Docker

A box around a program, with everything that program needs. The mail program runs in one box.

VPS, the front desk

A small computer you rent in a data center. “VPS” is the rental name. We call it the front desk.

DNS and MX

DNS is the address book. MX is the line that says where mail for your domain goes.

WireGuard

The tool that builds the private tunnel between the Pi and the front desk.

SPF

The public guest list. It names who may send mail for your domain. Ours names the front desk.

DKIM

The seal the Pi stamps on outgoing letters. Receivers check the seal against a public key in the address book.

DMARC

A public note that says what to do when the guest list or the seal fails. We start by asking for reports only.

TLS certificate

The lock. It proves the server is mail.example.com and keeps the letter private on the wire.

Reverse DNS

The question “who lives at this address?” answered with mail.example.com. The front desk’s company sets this.

Mailbox

One person’s pile of letters on the disk. alex@example.com is one mailbox.

The order

Build it in this order

Each step has something you can see. Stay on a step until that thing is true. The mail program comes after the door works.

1
Learn what the home line allows

Do the four checks. They decide whether you need the front desk. On a typical home line, you do.

2
Choose a domain, and a desk

Buy a name whose address book you can edit. Rent the front desk only from a company that will open the mail door and print the matching name.

3
Give the Pi a real disk

Plug in the USB SSD. Restart the Pi and confirm the disk is still there. Letters will be written to that disk.

4
Start the mail box, in private

Run the Docker program on the Pi. Make two test mailboxes. Send a letter from one to the other, inside the house, before the world is involved.

5
Publish the address book and the lock

Add the MX line, the guest list, the seal’s public key, and the DMARC note. Get the certificate for mail.example.com.

6
Send one letter to a real Gmail account

Read the details Gmail shows. The sending address should be the front desk. Then reply, and watch that reply land on the Pi.

7
Put it on your phone

Add the account in the normal mail app. Reading uses door 993. Sending uses door 587. Both ask for the password.

8
Make a backup, and open the backup

Copy the disk’s mail, the account list, and the seal’s private key somewhere else. Restore that copy once, and open a letter from it, before you depend on it.

Before we build

Four decisions, then we can start

The pictures stay the same. These answers change the details.

1

What domain do you want?

You need a DNS host that lets you edit MX and TXT records. SPF, DKIM, and DMARC are all TXT records.

2

Is a small rented computer okay?

It is often a few dollars a month. On a normal home connection, this is the decision that lets mail arrive and be trusted.

3

Who gets a mailbox?

One person is the simple start. A household means a password and a pile of letters for each person, plus a backup that matters.

4

Is the Pi free for this job?

A Pi 4 or 5 with 4 GB of memory is enough when mail is the main thing it does. If it already runs other things, 8 GB is the comfortable size.
